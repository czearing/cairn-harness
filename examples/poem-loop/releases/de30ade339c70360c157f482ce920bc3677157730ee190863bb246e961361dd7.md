The Clockmaker's Return

They called her from the quiet of her chair
when every clock in town forgot to move,
same frozen minute caught in every square,
as if the hours stopped, waiting to prove
that time can rest, and someone still must come
to coax it back with patient, steady hands.

She opened first the case above the drum
where brass gears slept beneath the dust that stands
for years unwound. She knew this clock had chimed
the morning of a birth, the mother's cry
folded into ticking, softly timed
to match a small new breath against the sky.

Next door she found a case gone dim with age,
the one that marked a wedding long ago,
two names carved faintly on the inner page
where someone's thumb had worn a steady flow.
She cleaned the gears, she oiled every part,
and felt the pendulum begin to sway
like something waking gently in the heart,
a promise that had never slipped away.

The third house held a clock that once had kept
the hour of a goodbye at the door,
its hands grown still the night that no one slept,
yet still it waited, patient as before.
She touched the spring, she let the sunlight fall
through shop glass thick with years of quiet grain,
and felt the town's own breath rise through it all,
as ticking spread from house to house again.

She is not young, her fingers move more slow,
but every gear she frees begins to hum
like something small remembering to grow,
a small hand learning how to overcome.
And though she cannot make the past return,
she gives each home its pulse, its steady beat,
a quiet gift that lets the townsfolk learn
that even stopped time can be made complete.
